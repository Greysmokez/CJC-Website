Gathered Together in One

One People, One Harvest, One Gathering: Why God Carries His People Through the Storm

“And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad.” — John 11:52 (KJV)

Author’s Note on Terminology: This paper uses the traditional Christian terms “Old Testament” and “New Testament” for clarity. The author recognizes that the books of the Old Testament are the Hebrew Bible, the Tanakh, which Jewish tradition holds as complete Scripture in its own right, and quotes them with respect for that tradition.

A note on what follows. The interpretation in this paper is my best understanding of Scripture, reached by following its own examples and patterns. About This Paper

This paper is a companion to my study Does Scripture Support the Pre-Tribulation Theory? That paper tests the pre-tribulation view one doctrine at a time. This one takes a different road. It starts with a simple question: who are God’s people? Then it walks through the stories Scripture tells about how God has treated His people in times of trouble, from Jesus’ parables to Noah, the fiery furnace, Paul’s shipwreck and the last pages of Revelation. Along the way, Part 9 shows God’s Jubilee calendar running unbroken through Daniel’s seventy weeks, leaving no missing seven years to save for later.

Every teaching in these pages, including my own, is measured the same way. For each claim we will take three steps:

  • What the view teaches. The claim in its own terms, stated fairly, the way its own teachers would say it.
  • What Scripture says. The verses themselves, quoted in full.
  • Measured by Scripture. Does the claim agree with the text, or does it conflict with it? And why?

Where the pre-tribulation view agrees with Scripture, I will say so gladly. Where it conflicts, I will show the verses and let them speak.

Introduction: Rescued Before, or Carried Through?

Millions of Christians have been taught that one day, without warning, believers will vanish from the earth. Cars will be left driverless, planes will lose their pilots, and the people left behind will face seven terrible years under the Antichrist. Only after those seven years will Christ come back in glory for all to see.

That teaching is called the pre-tribulation rapture, “pre” because the church is taken away before the great tribulation begins. Many sincere and godly people hold it, and many came to faith through it. This paper is not written against them. It is written to ask one question and to let Scripture answer it:

When trouble comes at the end, will God take His people out before it, or carry His people through it and gather them when Christ appears?

What the view teaches

As its own teachers explain it, the pre-tribulation view holds that Christ will come secretly for His church before a seven-year tribulation. The dead in Christ will be raised, living believers will be changed, and all of them will be taken to heaven. On earth, the seven years follow: the Antichrist rises, God’s judgments fall, and God turns His attention back to the nation of Israel. At the end of the seven years Christ returns visibly, with His saints, to defeat His enemies and set up His kingdom.

At the root of the whole system is one idea: that God has two peoples, Israel and the church, with two separate plans. The church must be removed so that God can finish His plan for Israel. Pull up that root, and the rest of the system has nothing to stand on. That is why this paper begins where it does, with who God’s people are.

The measure we will use

God gave His people a standard for testing every teacher: “To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them” (Isaiah 8:20). The believers in Berea were praised for using it, even when the teacher was the apostle Paul: “These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so” (Acts 17:11).

That is the only measure in this paper. Not how popular a teaching is, not how many books it has sold, and not how sincere its teachers are. Only whether it agrees with what Scripture says.

Where the pre-tribulation view is right

Fairness starts with credit where it is due. The pre-tribulation view agrees with Scripture on several important things, and I agree with it on every one of them.

  • Christ is coming again, bodily and in glory. The angels promised the disciples: “this same Jesus, which is taken up from you into heaven, shall so come in like manner as ye have seen him go into heaven” (Acts 1:11).
  • God’s people are not appointed to His wrath. “For God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9).
  • Believers should watch and be ready. “Watch therefore: for ye know not what hour your Lord doth come” (Matthew 24:42).
  • God has not forgotten Israel. “I say then, Hath God cast away his people? God forbid” (Romans 11:1).

So the real disagreement is narrow. It is not about whether Christ is coming, and it is not about whether God’s people face His wrath. They do not. It is about when God gathers His people, and how He protects them until then.

What this paper will show

As I read Scripture, God’s people will be gathered once, at the last trump, when Christ appears in glory after the tribulation. Until then, God does what He has always done: He keeps His people through the trouble, not out of it. He seals them before the trumpets sound. He shelters them from His wrath as He sheltered Israel in Goshen while the plagues fell on Egypt. And then He gathers them all together, the living and the dead, in one great harvest.

That timing does not mean the church suffers God’s wrath. Persecution from the world and wrath from God are two different things, and Scripture keeps them apart. Jesus warned His followers, “In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world” (John 16:33). Tribulation comes from the world. Wrath comes from God, and it is never aimed at His own.

Why does any of this matter? Because what we expect shapes how we prepare. Jesus warned about a servant who says in his heart, “My lord delayeth his coming” (Matthew 24:48), and about ten virgins whose lamps ran low while “the bridegroom tarried” (Matthew 25:5). A believer who expects to be carried through will keep oil in the lamp. A believer who expects to be gone before trouble starts may not. My hope is that this paper helps every reader, whatever view they hold today, have a faith that lasts through the long night.

How a Teaching Became a Culture

Most Christians who hold the pre-tribulation view did not find it by reading Scripture alone. They met it in the notes of a study Bible, in a popular book, in a church film, or in a bestselling novel. It is worth knowing that history, not to mock it, but because it helps explain why a teaching that Scripture does not state became so widely believed. What follows is history, not Scripture, and I label it that way.

Before the 1800s

For most of church history, believers who expected Christ’s kingdom on earth looked for the gathering of the saints at His glorious appearing, at the end of the tribulation. Historians who have studied the period note that ideas about believers being spared the final trouble did appear here and there, but they were a minority view. A separate, secret coming of Christ seven years before His visible return was not the common expectation of the church.

John Nelson Darby

That changed in the 1830s. John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), a former Anglican clergyman and an early leader of the Plymouth Brethren in Britain, taught a new system for reading the Bible. He divided history into ages, or “dispensations,” and taught a sharp separation between Israel and the church. From that separation came his most distinctive teaching: a secret, any-moment rapture of the church before the tribulation. Historians debate how much of this Darby invented and how much he gathered from others, but nearly all agree that he systematized it and spread it more than anyone before him. His system came to be called dispensationalism, and sometimes simply “Darbyism.”

Bible conferences and the Scofield Bible

Darby traveled to North America several times, and his ideas spread through Bible conferences and new Bible institutes in the late 1800s. Then, in 1909, Cyrus I. Scofield, a minister associated with the evangelist Dwight L. Moody, published the Scofield Reference Bible. Its notes, printed on the same page as the biblical text, taught Darby’s system as the plain meaning of the verses above them. For millions of readers, the line between Scripture and the notes underneath it blurred. Seminaries and Bible colleges taught the system to generations of pastors.

Books and films

In 1970, Hal Lindsey’s The Late Great Planet Earth brought the pre-tribulation view into the mainstream, selling around fifteen million copies. In 1972, a low-budget film called A Thief in the Night was made for about $68,000 and shown in churches and youth groups for years, earning an estimated $4.2 million in its first decade. Many who saw it as children remember the fear it left behind. Its title comes from 1 Thessalonians 5:2, “the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night.” Yet two verses later Paul tells believers the opposite of what the film taught: “But ye, brethren, are not in darkness, that that day should overtake you as a thief” (1 Thessalonians 5:4). We will come back to that verse in Part 7.

Then, in 1995, Tim LaHaye and Jerry B. Jenkins released Left Behind, the first of sixteen novels. The series crossed over from Christian bookstores into the general market, seven of its titles reached the top of the bestseller charts, and by 2017 it had sold more than 65 million copies. Feature films followed in 2000, 2014 and 2023. For many people today, the pre-tribulation rapture is not a doctrine they studied. It is a story they have seen.

Measured by Scripture

None of this proves the teaching wrong, and none of it proves it right. The men behind these books and films were sincere believers who wanted people to come to Christ, and many did. But popularity is not the test, and neither is sincerity. “Let God be true, but every man a liar” (Romans 3:4).

There is one lesson from this history worth holding onto. A story told vividly enough can shape what we expect from Scripture before we ever open it. Even in 1998, at the height of Left Behind‘s success, a pastor who held the after-tribulation view called LaHaye a friend and a good Christian, yet warned that his teaching could leave people unprepared if bad times came. That concern is the reason for this paper. We will return to it in Part 10.

So let us set the stories aside and open the Book. The first question is the one Darby’s whole system rests on: does God have two peoples, or one?

Part 1. One Body, One Tree, One People

“There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling; One Lord, one faith, one baptism, One God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in you all.” — Ephesians 4:4-6

What the view teaches

In the dispensational system, Israel and the church are two distinct peoples of God with two distinct destinies. Israel is God’s earthly people, with earthly promises. The church is His heavenly people, a “mystery” the Old Testament prophets never saw. In this view, the church age is like a parenthesis in God’s plan for Israel. When the church is complete, God removes it from the earth so He can return to Israel and finish Daniel’s seventieth week with them during a seven-year tribulation.

What Scripture says

Paul answers the question with a picture anyone can understand: a single olive tree.

1. One olive tree

“And if some of the branches be broken off, and thou, being a wild olive tree, wert graffed in among them, and with them partakest of the root and fatness of the olive tree; Boast not against the branches. But if thou boast, thou bearest not the root, but the root thee” (Romans 11:17-18).

Look at what Paul describes. There is one tree, with one root. Some of Israel’s natural branches were broken off through unbelief. Gentile believers, branches from a wild olive, were grafted in among the branches that remained. They did not start a new tree. They were joined to Israel’s tree and share its root and its life.

And the story does not end there. “And they also, if they abide not still in unbelief, shall be graffed in: for God is able to graff them in again. For if thou wert cut out of the olive tree which is wild by nature, and wert graffed contrary to nature into a good olive tree: how much more shall these, which be the natural branches, be graffed into their own olive tree?” (Romans 11:23-24).

Notice the words “their own olive tree.” The tree belongs to Israel. When Jewish people come to faith in their Messiah, they are not moved to a different tree. They are grafted back into their own, alongside the Gentiles who were brought in. Paul’s conclusion: “And so all Israel shall be saved” (Romans 11:26).

This is not replacement theology. The church does not replace Israel. Israel is not cast off for good, “God hath not cast away his people which he foreknew” (Romans 11:2), and Gentile believers are told not to boast against the natural branches. There is one tree, and God’s plan is to fill it, with Jew and Gentile alike, by faith.

2. Children of Abraham by faith

If there is one tree, who belongs to it? Paul answers directly: “Know ye therefore that they which are of faith, the same are the children of Abraham” (Galatians 3:7). And a few verses later: “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus. And if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise” (Galatians 3:28-29).

Heirs of the promise. Not a second group with second promises, but heirs of the same promise God made to Abraham. Paul makes the same point from the other side in Romans: “For they are not all Israel, which are of Israel... That is, They which are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God: but the children of the promise are counted for the seed” (Romans 9:6, 8).

God’s family has always been defined by faith in His promise. That is how Abraham himself was counted righteous, and that is how every member of his family is counted, before the cross and after it.

3. One new man, one commonwealth, one fold

To the Gentile believers in Ephesus, Paul described what they had been before Christ: “That at that time ye were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world” (Ephesians 2:12).

Then he told them what had changed: “But now in Christ Jesus ye who sometimes were far off are made nigh by the blood of Christ. For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us” (Ephesians 2:13-14). Christ came “for to make in himself of twain one new man, so making peace” (Ephesians 2:15).

And the result: “Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19).

Gentile believers were once aliens from the commonwealth of Israel. Now they are fellow citizens. Not of a separate, heavenly commonwealth, but of the same household of God. Jesus said it in a shepherd’s words: “And other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd” (John 10:16). One fold. One Shepherd.

4. The “mystery” Paul revealed

The dispensational view calls the church a mystery the prophets never saw, a people separate from Israel. Paul does use the word mystery, and he tells us exactly what the mystery is: “Which in other ages was not made known unto the sons of men, as it is now revealed unto his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit; That the Gentiles should be fellowheirs, and of the same body, and partakers of his promise in Christ by the gospel” (Ephesians 3:5-6).

The mystery is not that God would form a second people. The mystery is that Gentiles would become fellow heirs, of the same body, sharing the same promise. Paul’s own definition of the mystery is one body, not two.

5. The door was always open

This was not a new idea in the New Testament. From the beginning, God’s people were defined by covenant and faith, not by bloodline alone. At the very first Passover, God said: “One law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among you” (Exodus 12:49).

Two of the most famous outsiders in the Old Testament joined Israel by faith. Rahab of Jericho hid Israel’s spies and was spared when the city fell. Her house stood in the very wall that came down, yet she and her family were kept safe inside the city while judgment fell all around them (Joshua 6:22-25), a picture we will come back to in Part 5. Ruth the Moabite told her mother-in-law, “whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God” (Ruth 1:16). Both women are named in the family line of Jesus Himself: “And Salmon begat Booz of Rachab; and Booz begat Obed of Ruth” (Matthew 1:5).

Through Isaiah, God promised that foreigners who joined themselves to Him would have a full place in His house: “Even them will I bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer... for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people” (Isaiah 56:7).

When the apostles met in Jerusalem to decide how Gentile believers fit into God’s plan, James did not describe a new and separate people. He described Israel being rebuilt, with the Gentiles inside it: “After this I will return, and will build again the tabernacle of David, which is fallen down; and I will build again the ruins thereof, and I will set it up: That the residue of men might seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, upon whom my name is called” (Acts 15:16-17). The Gentiles were not a parenthesis in Israel’s story. They were part of its restoration.

6. One covenant, one city

God promised a new covenant “with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah” (Jeremiah 31:31). The book of Hebrews quotes that very promise and applies it to believers in Christ (Hebrews 8:8-10). There are not two new covenants. There is one, made with Israel, and every believer enters it through Christ.

Peter took the titles God gave Israel at Mount Sinai and gave them to the scattered believers he was writing to: “But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people... Which in time past were not a people, but are now the people of God” (1 Peter 2:9-10).

And at the very end of the story, there is one city for one people. John saw the New Jerusalem: “And had a wall great and high, and had twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, and names written thereon, which are the names of the twelve tribes of the children of Israel” (Revelation 21:12). “And the wall of the city had twelve foundations, and in them the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb” (Revelation 21:14). The tribes of Israel on the gates. The apostles of the Lamb on the foundations. Every redeemed person walks through Israel’s gates and stands on the apostles’ foundation. One city.

7. One gathering

If God has one people, then He has one gathering. That is exactly what Caiaphas, the high priest, prophesied without knowing it, when he said Jesus would die for the nation: “And this spake he not of himself: but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus should die for that nation; And not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad” (John 11:51-52).

Gather together in one. Paul uses the same words for the goal of God’s whole plan: “That in the dispensation of the fulness of times he might gather together in one all things in Christ, both which are in heaven, and which are on earth; even in him” (Ephesians 1:10). There is the word dispensation in Scripture itself, and where it leads is one gathering, not two.

And listen to how Jesus describes the gathering at His coming: “And then shall he send his angels, and shall gather together his elect from the four winds, from the uttermost part of the earth to the uttermost part of heaven” (Mark 13:27). Those words echo God’s ancient promise to Israel: “If any of thine be driven out unto the outmost parts of heaven, from thence will the LORD thy God gather thee, and from thence will he fetch thee” (Deuteronomy 30:4). The gathering God promised scattered Israel and the gathering Jesus promised His elect are the same gathering, of the same people, at the same time.

Measured by Scripture

The claim that God has two peoples with two separate plans conflicts with Scripture. Paul shows one olive tree, with Gentiles grafted in and Israel’s natural branches grafted back into “their own olive tree.” He calls every believer Abraham’s seed and an heir of the promise. He says Christ made “both one,” and he defines the mystery itself as Gentiles being “of the same body.” Jesus speaks of one fold and one Shepherd. The new covenant is one covenant, made with Israel. The New Jerusalem has Israel’s tribes on its gates and the apostles on its foundations. And John 11:52 tells us why Christ died: to “gather together in one the children of God.”

This matters for everything that follows. If God has one people, then the “elect” Jesus gathers in Matthew 24 are the same people Paul writes to in his letters. There is no second people waiting for a separate rescue, and no reason to remove the church so that God can deal with Israel apart from it. What remains is one people, one harvest, and one gathering.

So how has God treated His one people when trouble came? Jesus answered that question Himself, in His parables. That is where we turn next.

Part 2. One Harvest: The Parables of Jesus

“Let both grow together until the harvest.” — Matthew 13:30

Jesus taught more about His coming in stories than in any other way. A farmer’s field, a fisherman’s net, a wedding at midnight, a master who travels far away and comes back. Each story is simple enough for a child to follow, and each one answers the same question: what will God’s people be doing, and where will they be, when He comes?

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view generally places the harvest parables at the end of the seven-year tribulation, after the church has already been taken. In that reading, the wheat gathered at the harvest and the sheep gathered at the throne are “tribulation saints,” people who came to faith after the rapture. Many teachers also hold that Matthew 24 and 25 were spoken mainly about Israel, so the “elect” and the watching servants in those chapters are not the church.

Who Jesus was speaking to

Before we read the parables, it helps to ask who they were for. Jesus spoke them to His disciples, the men who would become the foundation of the church: “And are built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ himself being the chief corner stone” (Ephesians 2:20). When He finished the parable of the watchful porter, He made the audience plain: “And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch” (Mark 13:37). And before He ascended, He told those same disciples to pass everything on to every nation: “Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world” (Matthew 28:20). These parables belong to the whole church, to the end of the world.

1. The wheat and the tares

“Another parable put he forth unto them, saying, The kingdom of heaven is likened unto a man which sowed good seed in his field: But while men slept, his enemy came and sowed tares among the wheat, and went his way” (Matthew 13:24-25).

When the servants saw the weeds coming up, they asked whether they should pull them out. The master said no: “Nay; lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them. Let both grow together until the harvest: and in the time of harvest I will say to the reapers, Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn” (Matthew 13:29-30).

We do not have to guess what the story means. Jesus explained it Himself: “He that soweth the good seed is the Son of man; The field is the world; the good seed are the children of the kingdom; but the tares are the children of the wicked one; The enemy that sowed them is the devil; the harvest is the end of the world; and the reapers are the angels. As therefore the tares are gathered and burned in the fire; so shall it be in the end of this world. The Son of man shall send forth his angels, and they shall gather out of his kingdom all things that offend, and them which do iniquity... Then shall the righteous shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father” (Matthew 13:37-41, 43).

Three things stand out.

  • Both grow together until the harvest. The master would not pull anything out of the field early. The wheat stays in the world, side by side with the weeds, until the harvest.
  • The harvest is the end of the world. Jesus says so in plain words. There is one harvest, at the end.
  • The tares are gathered first. The order is the reverse of the pre-tribulation picture. In that picture, the righteous are taken first and the wicked are left behind. In Jesus’ story, the wicked are gathered out first, and the wheat is brought into the barn.

The good seed was sown by the Son of man Himself, from the start of His ministry. The same wheat grows in the same field from the first sowing to the last harvest. There is no point in the story where the master removes his wheat early to protect it from the weeds. He protects it by letting it grow until he gathers it.

2. The dragnet

“Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a net, that was cast into the sea, and gathered of every kind: Which, when it was full, they drew to shore, and sat down, and gathered the good into vessels, but cast the bad away. So shall it be at the end of the world: the angels shall come forth, and sever the wicked from among the just” (Matthew 13:47-49).

The same lesson in a fisherman’s words. One net, one catch, one separation, “at the end of the world.” And notice the direction again: the angels “sever the wicked from among the just.” The wicked are taken out from among God’s people.

3. The sheep and the goats

“When the Son of man shall come in his glory, and all the holy angels with him, then shall he sit upon the throne of his glory: And before him shall be gathered all nations: and he shall separate them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats: And he shall set the sheep on his right hand, but the goats on the left. Then shall the King say unto them on his right hand, Come, ye blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world” (Matthew 25:31-34).

When does this gathering happen? “When the Son of man shall come in his glory, and all the holy angels with him.” That is the visible, glorious coming, the very coming the pre-tribulation view places seven years after the church is gone.

And what do the sheep receive? “The kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.” That is not a lesser inheritance for a second group. It is the same inheritance promised to every believer: “According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world” (Ephesians 1:4). The righteous are gathered and rewarded at the glorious coming, all of them together.

4. The pounds

Luke tells us exactly why Jesus told this parable: “he added and spake a parable, because he was nigh to Jerusalem, and because they thought that the kingdom of God should immediately appear. He said therefore, A certain nobleman went into a far country to receive for himself a kingdom, and to return” (Luke 19:11-12).

The nobleman leaves money with his servants and goes away. When he returns, two things happen. First his servants are called to account: “And it came to pass, that when he was returned, having received the kingdom, then he commanded these servants to be called unto him, to whom he had given the money, that he might know how much every man had gained by trading” (Luke 19:15). Then his enemies are judged: “But those mine enemies, which would not that I should reign over them, bring hither, and slay them before me” (Luke 19:27).

One return. At that one return, the servants are rewarded and the enemies are judged. Jesus did not describe a return for his servants and then a second return, seven years later, for his enemies.

5. The ten virgins

“Then shall the kingdom of heaven be likened unto ten virgins, which took their lamps, and went forth to meet the bridegroom” (Matthew 25:1).

Notice the first word: “Then.” Jesus is still answering the disciples’ question from Matthew 24, and “then” points back to what He just described, the coming of the Son of man in the clouds after the tribulation (Matthew 24:29-31). The chapter break between Matthew 24 and 25 was added many centuries later. Jesus spoke without stopping.

“While the bridegroom tarried, they all slumbered and slept. And at midnight there was a cry made, Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him. Then all those virgins arose, and trimmed their lamps. And the foolish said unto the wise, Give us of your oil; for our lamps are gone out” (Matthew 25:5-8).

All ten were waiting for the bridegroom. All ten had lamps. All ten fell asleep. What separated them was not whether they believed he was coming. It was whether they had brought enough oil for a long wait. The bridegroom “tarried,” and the foolish had prepared for a short night.

“And while they went to buy, the bridegroom came; and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut” (Matthew 25:10). One midnight cry. One door, shut once. No second opening seven years later.

And Jesus ends with the lesson: “Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh” (Matthew 25:13). The coming the virgins wait for is the coming of the Son of man, the same coming He has been describing since Matthew 24:30.

6. The faithful servant and the evil servant

Just before the virgins, Jesus told of two servants: “Who then is a faithful and wise servant, whom his lord hath made ruler over his household, to give them meat in due season? Blessed is that servant, whom his lord when he cometh shall find so doing” (Matthew 24:45-46).

The faithful servant’s job is to feed the household “in due season,” day after day, for as long as the master is away. The danger Jesus warns about is not a servant who expects his master too late. It is a servant who decides the wait is longer than he planned for, and stops preparing: “But and if that evil servant shall say in his heart, My lord delayeth his coming; And shall begin to smite his fellowservants, and to eat and drink with the drunken; The lord of that servant shall come in a day when he looketh not for him” (Matthew 24:48-50).

In the parable of the talents, Jesus says the same thing in five words: “After a long time the lord of those servants cometh” (Matthew 25:19). Jesus prepared His people for a long wait, and for faithfulness all the way through it.

7. The seed on stony ground

In the parable of the sower, one kind of hearer receives the word gladly, but does not last: “But he that received the seed into stony places, the same is he that heareth the word, and anon with joy receiveth it; Yet hath he not root in himself, but dureth for a while: for when tribulation or persecution ariseth because of the word, by and by he is offended” (Matthew 13:20-21).

This is one of the most important warnings in this paper. Jesus said tribulation and persecution would come “because of the word,” and that faith without deep roots would not survive it. A faith that expects to be removed before trouble comes is a faith that has not put its roots down for trouble.

8. The two builders

“Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock: And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock” (Matthew 7:24-25).

The foolish man built on sand, and the same storm came: “and the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell: and great was the fall of it” (Matthew 7:27).

The storm hit both houses. Faith did not keep the storm away from the wise man’s house. Faith decided which house was still standing when the storm was over.

9. The widow who would not give up

Jesus told this parable “to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint” (Luke 18:1). A widow kept coming to an unjust judge until he gave her justice. Then Jesus said: “And shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them? I tell you that he will avenge them speedily. Nevertheless when the Son of man cometh, shall he find faith on the earth?” (Luke 18:7-8).

God’s elect are crying to Him “day and night.” He bears long with them. And when the Son of man comes, He looks for faith “on the earth.” The parable assumes His people are still here, still praying, still holding on, when He comes.

10. The fig tree and the watchful porter

“Now learn a parable of the fig tree; When his branch is yet tender, and putteth forth leaves, ye know that summer is nigh: So likewise ye, when ye shall see all these things, know that it is near, even at the doors” (Matthew 24:32-33).

Jesus gave His people signs to watch: “when ye shall see all these things.” That only makes sense if they will be here to see them. A coming with no signs before it, at any moment, has no need of a fig tree.

And to the porter at the door, Jesus said: “Watch ye therefore: for ye know not when the master of the house cometh, at even, or at midnight, or at the cockcrowing, or in the morning: Lest coming suddenly he find you sleeping. And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch” (Mark 13:35-37). The porter is told to watch through every watch of the night: evening, midnight, cockcrow, morning. Not one short watch, but the whole night.

Measured by Scripture

The claim that the parables describe a harvest after the church is already gone conflicts with Scripture. Jesus’ own explanations leave no room for it:

  • The wheat and the tares grow together “until the harvest,” and “the harvest is the end of the world.”
  • The wicked are gathered out first, not the righteous.
  • The sheep are gathered “when the Son of man shall come in his glory,” and they inherit the kingdom prepared “from the foundation of the world.”
  • The nobleman rewards his servants and judges his enemies at one return.
  • The virgins wait through a long night for one coming, and the door is shut once.
  • The servants are warned against “My lord delayeth,” and the stony ground warns that faith without roots fails “when tribulation or persecution ariseth.”
  • The house on the rock stands through the storm.
  • The elect are still crying out “on the earth” when He comes.

And Jesus told all of it to the men who became the foundation of the church, saying, “what I say unto you I say unto all.”

None of this means the church suffers God’s wrath. The harvest parables are about separation and gathering, and in every one of them the righteous are kept safe and gathered in. The tares go to the fire. The wheat goes to the barn.

So what did Jesus say His coming would be like? He chose two pictures from the Old Testament. That is where we turn next.

Part 3. As It Was in the Days of Noah and Lot

“But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be.” — Matthew 24:37

When Jesus wanted to show what His coming would be like, He did not have to invent an example. He pointed to two that every listener knew: the flood in Noah’s day, and the fire that fell on Sodom in Lot’s day.

What the view teaches

Many pre-tribulation teachers see Enoch, who was taken to heaven without dying, as a picture of the church raptured before judgment. Noah, who went through the flood in the ark, is seen as a picture of Israel, or of a remnant preserved through the tribulation. The words “one shall be taken, and the other left” (Matthew 24:40) are often read as the rapture: believers taken, unbelievers left behind. And God’s words to Lot, “I cannot do any thing till thou be come thither” (Genesis 19:22), are read to mean that God cannot pour out judgment until His people are removed.

1. The days of Noah

Here is what Jesus said: “But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be. For as in the days that were before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day that Noe entered into the ark, And knew not until the flood came, and took them all away; so shall also the coming of the Son of man be. Then shall two be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other left. Two women shall be grinding at the mill; the one shall be taken, and the other left” (Matthew 24:37-41).

First, notice which coming Jesus is describing. Twice He calls it “the coming of the Son of man.” He has just described that coming a few verses earlier: “Immediately after the tribulation of those days... they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory. And he shall send his angels with a great sound of a trumpet, and they shall gather together his elect” (Matthew 24:29-31). The days of Noah describe the coming that happens after the tribulation. There is no other coming in the chapter.

Second, notice who was taken in Noah’s day. “The flood came, and took them all away.” The ones swept away were the wicked. Noah was not taken. He was kept.

Then Jesus says, “Then shall two be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other left.” Here something important happens in the Greek. The word for “took... away” in verse 39, where the flood swept away the wicked, is airō, to lift up and carry off. But the word for “taken” in verse 40 is a different word, paralambanō, which means to take alongside, to receive to oneself.

Elsewhere, Scripture uses this very word for Christ receiving His own. It is the word in Jesus’ promise to His disciples: “I will come again, and receive you unto myself; that where I am, there ye may be also” (John 14:3). It is the word used when Jesus “taketh Peter, James, and John his brother” up the mountain to see His glory (Matthew 17:1), and when the angel told Joseph to “take the young child and his mother, and flee into Egypt” (Matthew 2:13), taking the child Jesus to safety. As I read it, Jesus changed words on purpose. The flood took the wicked away in judgment, but at His coming, the one taken is received by Him, gathered as the angels “gather together his elect” just a few verses earlier (Matthew 24:31), and the one left behind is left for judgment.

To be fair, the same word can describe being taken by force, as when the soldiers “took Jesus, and led him away” to be crucified (John 19:16), and some teachers point to Luke’s account, where the disciples asked, “Where, Lord?” and Jesus answered, “Wheresoever the body is, thither will the eagles be gathered together” (Luke 17:37), and read the taking as judgment. So the context has to decide, and Scripture does not settle every detail for us. But this paper does not depend on that detail. Either way the words are read, the event is the same: the coming of the Son of man after the tribulation. Whoever is taken, they are taken when He appears, not seven years before.

Third, notice how Noah was saved. God did not lift Noah off the earth before the flood. He brought him into the ark on the very day it began: “In the selfsame day entered Noah, and Shem, and Ham, and Japheth, the sons of Noah, and Noah’s wife, and the three wives of his sons with them, into the ark... and the LORD shut him in” (Genesis 7:13, 16). Then the ark carried them through it: “and the waters increased, and bare up the ark, and it was lift up above the earth” (Genesis 7:17). Peter says they were “saved by water” (1 Peter 3:20). Not saved from the water, but carried through it, above it, safe inside what God provided.

And Noah’s salvation took long preparation. “By faith Noah, being warned of God of things not seen as yet, moved with fear, prepared an ark to the saving of his house” (Hebrews 11:7). He built for years, believing God’s warning before there was any sign of rain. That is the oil in the lamp. That is the faith Jesus wants His people to have.

2. The days of Lot

Luke records the second picture: “Likewise also as it was in the days of Lot; they did eat, they drank, they bought, they sold, they planted, they builded; But the same day that Lot went out of Sodom it rained fire and brimstone from heaven, and destroyed them all. Even thus shall it be in the day when the Son of man is revealed” (Luke 17:28-30).

“The same day.” Lot’s rescue and Sodom’s judgment happened on one day. And Jesus says it will be “even thus... in the day when the Son of man is revealed.” Revealed, not hidden. The day of deliverance is the day the Son of man appears for all to see.

What about God’s words, “I cannot do any thing till thou be come thither”? They are true, and they are precious. God would not let His judgment fall on Lot. But read the whole verse and the verses around it. The angels took Lot out “when the morning arose” (Genesis 19:15), and “The sun was risen upon the earth when Lot entered into Zoar. Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire” (Genesis 19:23-24). God’s protection held Lot until the last hour, and then the judgment fell, the same day, not seven years later.

Lot was not spared from trouble before that day. Peter tells us that “just Lot” was “vexed with the filthy conversation of the wicked: (For that righteous man dwelling among them, in seeing and hearing, vexed his righteous soul from day to day with their unlawful deeds;)” (2 Peter 2:7-8). He lived through years of Sodom’s evil. What God spared him from was Sodom’s judgment. Peter draws the lesson: “The Lord knoweth how to deliver the godly out of temptations, and to reserve the unjust unto the day of judgment to be punished” (2 Peter 2:9).

And Jesus added a warning in three words: “Remember Lot’s wife” (Luke 17:32). She was rescued from the city, but her heart was still in it. She looked back, and she was lost. Being near the rescue is not the same as being ready for it.

3. What about Enoch?

“And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). Enoch’s story is beautiful, and it does show something real: God can take a living person to Himself without death. “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death” (Hebrews 11:5).

But three things are worth noticing.

  • Jesus never used Enoch as a picture of His coming. He chose Noah and Lot. When the Lord Himself picks the example, we should be careful about putting a different one in its place.
  • Enoch was not taken shortly before the flood. Counting the ages given in Genesis 5, Enoch was taken 669 years before the flood came. His son Methuselah was 300 when Enoch was taken, lived to 969, and died in the year the flood began, when Noah was 600 (Genesis 5:21-27; 7:6). His story is not a pattern of rescue seven years ahead of judgment.
  • Scripture does tell us when living believers will be taken up without dying, like Enoch. “Behold, I shew you a mystery; We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed, In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump” (1 Corinthians 15:51-52). Enoch is a beautiful picture of the living saints who will be changed without dying. Paul places that moment “at the last trump.”

Measured by Scripture

The claim that Noah and Lot picture a rescue seven years before judgment conflicts with Scripture. Jesus used both stories to describe “the coming of the Son of man,” the coming He placed “immediately after the tribulation.” In Noah’s day, the flood “took them all away,” the wicked, while Noah was carried through in the ark, brought in “the selfsame day.” In Lot’s day, rescue and fire came on “the same day,” and Jesus said it will be “even thus... in the day when the Son of man is revealed.” The claim that Enoch pictures the church is a reading Jesus never gave, and Scripture places the taking up of the living “at the last trump.”

Here is where the pre-tribulation view is right: God will not pour His wrath on His people. He did not let the flood touch Noah, and He did not let the fire touch Lot. That is exactly the point. He kept them in the middle of the trouble, and He delivered them on the day the judgment fell. “For God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9).

Noah was carried through the waters. In Babylon, three young men would be carried through the fire. That is where we turn next.

Part 4. Kept in the Fire

“When thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned; neither shall the flame kindle upon thee.” — Isaiah 43:2

The book of Daniel was written for God’s people living under a hostile empire. Its stories are not only history. They are lessons for the end, because Daniel’s own prophecies reach “to the time of the end” (Daniel 12:4), and Revelation borrows its pictures again and again: the beasts, the image, the decree, the ten horns. If we want to know how God keeps His people when a world power turns against them, Daniel shows us.

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view holds that God will remove His church before the great persecution of the end. A favorite verse is Jesus’ promise to the church in Philadelphia: “Because thou hast kept the word of my patience, I also will keep thee from the hour of temptation, which shall come upon all the world, to try them that dwell upon the earth” (Revelation 3:10). In this reading, “keep thee from” means taking the church out of the world before that hour begins.

A word about “keep thee from”

The same apostle John recorded the same two words, “keep from,” in the prayer Jesus prayed for His disciples the night before the cross: “I pray not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil” (John 17:15). There, Jesus says plainly that keeping them from the evil does not mean taking them out of the world. He asks the Father to keep them safe while they stay in it. My Pre-Tribulation paper looks at Revelation 3:10 in detail. Here, let us watch how God has actually kept His people when the hour of trial came.

1. The fiery furnace

King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon set up a golden image on the plain of Dura and gathered every official in his empire to worship it. His herald announced the law: “fall down and worship the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar the king hath set up: And whoso falleth not down and worshippeth shall the same hour be cast into the midst of a burning fiery furnace” (Daniel 3:5-6).

Three young Jewish men, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, would not bow. Brought before the furious king, they gave one of the bravest answers in Scripture: “If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up” (Daniel 3:17-18).

“But if not.” Their faith did not depend on being rescued ahead of time. They were ready to stand whether God kept them out of the fire or not.

God did not keep them out of the fire. He met them in it. The king, looking into the furnace, cried out: “Did not we cast three men bound into the midst of the fire?... Lo, I see four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire, and they have no hurt; and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God” (Daniel 3:24-25). When they came out, the fire had no power on their bodies, “nor was an hair of their head singed, neither were their coats changed, nor the smell of fire had passed on them” (Daniel 3:27).

Now set this story beside Revelation. At the end, a false prophet deceives the world, “saying to them that dwell on the earth, that they should make an image to the beast... and cause that as many as would not worship the image of the beast should be killed” (Revelation 13:14-15). An image. A command to worship it. A death sentence for anyone who refuses. As I read it, Daniel 3 is a small picture of the very trial the end will bring, and God’s answer in Daniel 3 was not to remove His servants before the image was set up. It was to stand with them in the fire.

And notice who the fire did hurt. The soldiers who threw the three men in were killed by the flames (Daniel 3:22). The fire that could not touch God’s servants destroyed the men who carried out the king’s command. That is how God’s judgment works: aimed with perfect precision.

2. Daniel in the lions’ den

Years later, under a new empire, jealous officials tricked King Darius into signing a law: “whosoever shall ask a petition of any God or man for thirty days, save of thee, O king, he shall be cast into the den of lions” (Daniel 6:7). Again, a decree about worship, backed by death.

Daniel did not hide and did not change his habits. He went home, opened his windows toward Jerusalem, and prayed three times a day, “as he did aforetime” (Daniel 6:10). He was thrown into the den. In the morning he called up to the king: “My God hath sent his angel, and hath shut the lions’ mouths, that they have not hurt me” (Daniel 6:22).

Daniel was kept in the den, not from it. And once again, the judgment fell precisely: the men who had accused him were thrown to the same lions (Daniel 6:24).

3. Esther and the decree of death

In the days of the Persian king Ahasuerus, a powerful man named Haman plotted to wipe out every Jew in the empire. He had a law sealed with the king’s ring, sent to every province, “to destroy, to kill, and to cause to perish, all Jews, both young and old, little children and women, in one day, even upon the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, which is the month Adar” (Esther 3:13).

God’s people were not taken out of Persia before the decree. They lived under it, with the date fixed and coming closer. Esther, the Jewish queen, had to choose whether to risk her life by going to the king uninvited. Her cousin Mordecai told her, “who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?” (Esther 4:14). Her answer: “so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish” (Esther 4:16).

God turned the decree upside down, on the very day it was meant to be carried out: “in the day that the enemies of the Jews hoped to have power over them, (though it was turned to the contrary, that the Jews had rule over them that hated them;)” (Esther 9:1).

Revelation tells of a death decree at the end, against all who will not worship the image (Revelation 13:15), and Daniel promises deliverance in that same hour: “and at that time thy people shall be delivered, every one that shall be found written in the book” (Daniel 12:1). As I read it, Esther’s story is that promise in miniature. God’s people live under the decree, and God turns it back on the very day their enemies hoped to destroy them.

4. Elijah in the famine

In the days of wicked King Ahab and Queen Jezebel, Elijah stood before the king and announced that no rain would fall except at his word (1 Kings 17:1). James tells us how long the drought lasted: “it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months” (James 5:17). Three and a half years, the same span Revelation gives at the end for the testimony of the two witnesses and the rule of the beast: 1,260 days, or forty-two months (Revelation 11:3; 13:5).

God did not take Elijah out of Israel during those years. He fed him in the middle of the famine. First by a brook: “And the ravens brought him bread and flesh in the morning, and bread and flesh in the evening; and he drank of the brook” (1 Kings 17:6). Then in the home of a poor widow, whose last handful of flour and little oil God multiplied: “The barrel of meal shall not waste, neither shall the cruse of oil fail, until the day that the LORD sendeth rain upon the earth” (1 Kings 17:14). Notice that the oil did not run out. God kept it flowing every day until the trial was over.

Elijah was not the only one kept. While Jezebel hunted down the prophets, a faithful man named Obadiah “took an hundred prophets, and hid them by fifty in a cave, and fed them with bread and water” (1 Kings 18:4). And when Elijah thought he was the last faithful man left, God told him, “Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal” (1 Kings 19:18).

At the end of those years came the test on Mount Carmel, where the God who answered by fire would be known as the true God, “Then the fire of the LORD fell” (1 Kings 18:38). Revelation shows the same test at the end, but with a counterfeit: a false prophet who “maketh fire come down from heaven on the earth in the sight of men” (Revelation 13:13) to deceive the world. And Revelation’s two witnesses carry Elijah’s own power: “These have power to shut heaven, that it rain not in the days of their prophecy” (Revelation 11:6).

God’s promise to the faithful in such times is the same one He kept for Elijah: “Bread shall be given him; his waters shall be sure” (Isaiah 33:16). And Elijah was taken up to heaven only after his work was done (2 Kings 2:11), not before the famine began.

Measured by Scripture

The claim that God’s pattern is to remove His people before persecution and trial conflicts with Scripture. In every one of these stories, God’s people lived under the hostile power, faced the decree, and were kept in the middle of it:

  • The three young men were kept in the fire, with the Son of God beside them.
  • Daniel was kept in the den, and the lions’ mouths were shut.
  • Esther and her people lived under a dated death decree, and God turned it on the very day.
  • Elijah was fed through three and a half years of famine, and the oil never ran out.

And Jesus Himself asked the Father “not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil.”

Here again the pre-tribulation view is right about one thing: God protects His own. In every story, the fire, the lions and the decree fell on the enemies of God’s people, not on His people. That is the difference between persecution and wrath. Persecution comes from a king, a queen or a beast. Wrath comes from God, and He aims it with perfect precision. “For God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9).

The fire and the lions came from men. What about when the plagues come from God Himself? For that, Scripture gives us the greatest deliverance story of all.

Part 5. Kept in the Plagues

“And I will put a division between my people and thy people.” — Exodus 8:23

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view holds that the whole seven-year tribulation is a time of God’s wrath, and since believers are not appointed to wrath, the church must be removed before any of it begins. A verse often given in support is Isaiah 26:20: “Come, my people, enter thou into thy chambers, and shut thy doors about thee: hide thyself as it were for a little moment, until the indignation be overpast.” In this reading, the “chambers” are heaven, and God’s people are hidden there while the indignation falls on earth.

1. The Exodus: Goshen through the plagues

When God judged Egypt, His people were not in some other land. They lived in Goshen, inside Egypt, while ten plagues fell around them. They had already suffered years of hard bondage, and when Moses first spoke to Pharaoh, their burdens got worse, not better (Exodus 5:6-9). Trouble from Pharaoh came on everyone. But when the judgments from God began to fall, God drew a line.

At the plague of flies, God said: “And I will sever in that day the land of Goshen, in which my people dwell, that no swarms of flies shall be there; to the end thou mayest know that I am the LORD in the midst of the earth. And I will put a division between my people and thy people” (Exodus 8:22-23).

Then plague after plague:

  • The cattle: “there shall nothing die of all that is the children’s of Israel” (Exodus 9:4).
  • The hail: “Only in the land of Goshen, where the children of Israel were, was there no hail” (Exodus 9:26).
  • The darkness: “but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings” (Exodus 10:23).

God’s people lived in the land under judgment, and the judgments did not touch them. God’s purpose was stated plainly: “that ye may know how that the LORD doth put a difference between the Egyptians and Israel” (Exodus 11:7). Only after the last plague did they leave: “even the selfsame day it came to pass, that all the hosts of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt” (Exodus 12:41).

Revelation draws straight from this story. The seven bowls, God’s final plagues, read like Egypt’s plagues again: painful sores (Revelation 16:2, compare Exodus 9:10), water turned to blood (Revelation 16:3-4, compare Exodus 7:20), darkness (Revelation 16:10, compare Exodus 10:22), and great hail (Revelation 16:21, compare Exodus 9:23-24). And the bowls are aimed just as precisely: the first one falls “upon the men which had the mark of the beast, and upon them which worshipped his image” (Revelation 16:2). The faithful who overcame the beast are pictured singing “the song of Moses the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb” (Revelation 15:3). The Exodus is the pattern, and Revelation tells us so.

2. The Passover and “enter thou into thy chambers”

The last plague in Egypt was the worst, the death of the firstborn. God told every family in Israel to kill a lamb and put its blood on the doorposts of their house: “And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you, when I smite the land of Egypt” (Exodus 12:13).

Then came the command: “and none of you shall go out at the door of his house until the morning. For the LORD will pass through to smite the Egyptians; and when he seeth the blood upon the lintel, and on the two side posts, the LORD will pass over the door, and will not suffer the destroyer to come in unto your houses to smite you” (Exodus 12:22-23).

Now read Isaiah 26:20 again with that night in mind: “Come, my people, enter thou into thy chambers, and shut thy doors about thee: hide thyself as it were for a little moment, until the indignation be overpast.” Go inside. Shut the door. Stay hidden until the destroyer has passed. That is the Passover. And the next verse tells us where the indignation falls: “For, behold, the LORD cometh out of his place to punish the inhabitants of the earth for their iniquity” (Isaiah 26:21).

Some teachers read the “chambers” as heaven, but the passage does not say heaven. As I read it, the picture Isaiah draws is the one every Israelite knew by heart: God’s people sheltered in their own homes, on the earth, under the blood, while His judgment passes over them “for a little moment.” That is why Paul could write, “For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us” (1 Corinthians 5:7). The blood of the Lamb is the shelter.

3. Ezekiel’s mark

In a vision, the prophet Ezekiel watched God prepare judgment on Jerusalem for its idolatry. But before the judgment began, God sent a man with a writer’s inkhorn: “And the LORD said unto him, Go through the midst of the city, through the midst of Jerusalem, and set a mark upon the foreheads of the men that sigh and that cry for all the abominations that be done in the midst thereof” (Ezekiel 9:4).

Then the executioners were sent out with a strict command: “Go ye after him through the city, and smite: let not your eye spare, neither have ye pity... but come not near any man upon whom is the mark” (Ezekiel 9:5-6).

The faithful were marked on their foreheads, in the city, and judgment passed them by. Revelation repeats the scene almost word for word. Before the trumpets sound, an angel cries, “Hurt not the earth, neither the sea, nor the trees, till we have sealed the servants of our God in their foreheads” (Revelation 7:3). And when the locusts come up from the pit, “it was commanded them that they should not hurt the grass of the earth, neither any green thing, neither any tree; but only those men which have not the seal of God in their foreheads” (Revelation 9:4). God marks His people where they are, and the judgment is told to pass them by.

4. Rahab and the scarlet cord

Rahab lived in Jericho, the city God had appointed for judgment. “Her house was upon the town wall, and she dwelt upon the wall” (Joshua 2:15). She had hidden Israel’s spies, and before they left, they gave her a sign: “thou shalt bind this line of scarlet thread in the window which thou didst let us down by: and thou shalt bring thy father, and thy mother, and thy brethren, and all thy father’s household, home unto thee. And it shall be, that whosoever shall go out of the doors of thy house into the street, his blood shall be upon his head” (Joshua 2:18-19).

A red sign in the window, a family gathered inside, and no one to go out the door. It is the Passover again.

Then Israel marched around Jericho, once a day for six days. On the seventh day they marched seven times, with seven priests blowing seven trumpets, and at the final blast and shout, the wall fell (Joshua 6:15-20). Only then did Joshua send the spies to “Go into the harlot’s house, and bring out thence the woman, and all that she hath” (Joshua 6:22). Rahab was kept in the city through all seven days and every trumpet blast, and she was brought out at the end, when the last trumpet sounded and the walls fell. “By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not” (Hebrews 11:31).

As I read it, the parallel with Revelation’s seven trumpets is hard to miss, and it points the same way as everything else: God’s people are kept inside the city under His sign, and gathered out when the last trumpet sounds.

5. Psalm 91

“He that dwelleth in the secret place of the most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty” (Psalm 91:1). This psalm has comforted believers through wars, plagues and persecutions for three thousand years, and it describes exactly what we have seen in every story: “Thou shalt not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flieth by day; Nor for the pestilence that walketh in darkness; nor for the destruction that wasteth at noonday. A thousand shall fall at thy side, and ten thousand at thy right hand; but it shall not come nigh thee. Only with thine eyes shalt thou behold and see the reward of the wicked” (Psalm 91:5-8).

“Only with thine eyes shalt thou behold.” The one who trusts God sees the judgment fall, because he is there. A thousand fall at his side. But it does not come near him. And God makes the promise personal: “He shall call upon me, and I will answer him: I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him, and honour him” (Psalm 91:15). Not “I will take him away before trouble.” “I will be with him in trouble.”

Measured by Scripture

The claim that God must remove His people before His judgments fall conflicts with Scripture. When God judged Egypt, He kept Israel in Goshen and drew “a division between my people and thy people.” On the night of the last plague, He sheltered them in their own houses under the blood, the very picture Isaiah 26:20 draws: “enter thou into thy chambers, and shut thy doors... until the indignation be overpast.” In Ezekiel’s vision and in Revelation, God marks His servants on their foreheads and commands the judgment, “come not near any man upon whom is the mark.” Rahab was kept in Jericho under the scarlet cord through all seven days and seven trumpets, and brought out when the walls fell. And Psalm 91 promises, “I will be with him in trouble.”

So here is the heart of the matter, and it is where the pre-tribulation view and Scripture agree: God’s wrath is not for His people. The bowls fall “upon the men which had the mark of the beast.” What Scripture shows is not that God removes His people so that His wrath can fall, but that He shelters them while it falls, and leads them out when it is finished.

Israel left Egypt the morning after the last plague, and Rahab came out when the last trumpet sounded. But some of God’s people have been called to endure long seasons of hardship that came from no single plague or decree at all: famine, siege and storm. That is where we turn next.

Part 6. Kept Through the Storm

“I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him, and honour him.” — Psalm 91:15

Not every trial in Scripture came as a plague or a decree. Some came as long seasons of hardship: famine, siege, and storm. These stories add one more piece to the pattern. God does not only shelter His people from His judgments. He provides for them and carries them through hardships that fall on everyone around them.

1. Joseph’s seven years

The Bible does record a seven-year time of trouble. It came in Joseph’s day. God showed Pharaoh in a dream that “there come seven years of great plenty throughout all the land of Egypt: And there shall arise after them seven years of famine” (Genesis 41:29-30).

God did not remove His people before the famine. He prepared for them in the middle of it. “And the dearth was in all lands; but in all the land of Egypt there was bread” (Genesis 41:54). Jacob’s family came down to Egypt and lived through the famine years on the grain God had stored through Joseph. Joseph explained it to his brothers: “And God sent me before you to preserve you a posterity in the earth, and to save your lives by a great deliverance” (Genesis 45:7).

The one seven-year trial Scripture actually describes is a story of God feeding His people through it, not taking them away before it.

2. Hezekiah and the two unsown years

In the days of King Hezekiah, the Assyrian army swept through Judah, captured its fortified cities, and surrounded Jerusalem (2 Kings 18:13, 17). No one could go out to plant the fields. Hezekiah spread the enemy’s threatening letter before the LORD and prayed (2 Kings 19:14-19).

God answered through the prophet Isaiah with a sign: “And this shall be a sign unto thee, Ye shall eat this year such things as grow of themselves, and in the second year that which springeth of the same; and in the third year sow ye, and reap, and plant vineyards, and eat the fruits thereof” (2 Kings 19:29). For two years the people would live on what grew by itself, and God would make it enough. Then God promised: “He shall not come into this city, nor shoot an arrow there” (2 Kings 19:32). That very night, “the angel of the LORD went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand” (2 Kings 19:35).

God’s people stayed inside the besieged city. God fed them through it and delivered them in one night.

That sign of two unsown years in a row matters for another reason too. Under God’s law, the land rested every seventh year, and in the Jubilee, the fiftieth year, it rested again (Leviticus 25:4, 11). Two years in a row without sowing is what a sabbath year followed by a Jubilee looks like. On the Continuous Jubilee Calendar, that places a sabbath year in 703 BC and a Jubilee in 702 BC, an anchor point we will use in Part 9.

3. The storm on the sea, and Paul’s shipwreck

One evening Jesus told His disciples, “Let us pass over unto the other side” (Mark 4:35). He led them into the boat, and into the storm. “And there arose a great storm of wind, and the waves beat into the ship, so that it was now full. And he was in the hinder part of the ship, asleep on a pillow” (Mark 4:37-38). Terrified, they woke Him. “And he arose, and rebuked the wind, and said unto the sea, Peace, be still. And the wind ceased, and there was a great calm. And he said unto them, Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?” (Mark 4:39-40).

Jesus did not keep the storm from coming. He was in the boat with them through it. What He rebuked was not that the storm came, but that they were afraid while He was with them.

Years later Paul sailed as a prisoner toward Rome, and a violent storm drove the ship for fourteen days. “And when neither sun nor stars in many days appeared, and no small tempest lay on us, all hope that we should be saved was then taken away” (Acts 27:20). Then Paul stood up with a message from God: “And now I exhort you to be of good cheer: for there shall be no loss of any man’s life among you, but of the ship. For there stood by me this night the angel of God, whose I am, and whom I serve, Saying, Fear not, Paul... Wherefore, sirs, be of good cheer: for I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me” (Acts 27:22-25).

The ship was lost. Every person on it was saved. “And so it came to pass, that they escaped all safe to land” (Acts 27:44). There may be no clearer picture in Scripture of what it means to be kept through and not taken out.

4. The flight from Jerusalem

Jesus gave His disciples a warning about Jerusalem’s coming destruction: “And when ye shall see Jerusalem compassed with armies, then know that the desolation thereof is nigh. Then let them which are in Judaea flee to the mountains” (Luke 21:20-21). He did not promise to remove them before the armies came. He gave them a sign to watch for, and told them what to do when they saw it.

History records what happened, and I give it as history, not Scripture. The early church historian Eusebius wrote that the believers in Jerusalem, warned beforehand, left the city and settled in Pella, east of the Jordan, before the Romans destroyed it in AD 70. They were saved by watching for the sign and obeying it.

Measured by Scripture

The idea that God’s pattern is to remove His people before hard times conflicts with Scripture. In the Bible’s own seven-year famine, God fed His people through it. In Hezekiah’s siege, He fed them inside the walls and delivered them in a night. On the sea and in Paul’s shipwreck, He was with His people in the storm and brought every one of them safe to land. And before Jerusalem fell, Jesus gave His followers a sign to watch, not a promise of early removal.

Every one of these stories is a picture of the promise God gave through Isaiah: “When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee” (Isaiah 43:2). Through, not around.

We have seen the pattern in Jesus’ parables, in the flood and Sodom, in Babylon and Persia, in Egypt and Jericho, in famine, siege and storm. Now we come to the book that tells the end of the story. What does Revelation itself show?

Part 7. Revelation’s Own Picture

“These are they which came out of great tribulation, and have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.” — Revelation 7:14

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view holds that the church is raptured before the events of Revelation 6 through 19. Teachers often point out that the word “church” appears often in Revelation 2 and 3 but not in the chapters describing the tribulation, and they read Revelation 4:1, “Come up hither,” as a picture of the rapture. In this view, the believers who appear on earth during those chapters are “tribulation saints,” people who came to faith after the church was gone.

1. Written to the churches, about the saints

Revelation never stops being written to the church. Jesus says so at the end of the book: “I Jesus have sent mine angel to testify unto you these things in the churches” (Revelation 22:16). Every chapter, including the hard ones, was sent to the churches for them to read and keep.

The word “church” may not appear in the middle chapters, but God’s people are everywhere in them, under Scripture’s other names: “the saints” (Revelation 13:7, 10; 14:12; 16:6; 17:6), and those “which keep the commandments of God, and have the testimony of Jesus Christ” (Revelation 12:17). The word “church” does not appear in Revelation 19 or 21 either, where the bride and the holy city are pictured, yet no one doubts the church is there.

And “Come up hither” was spoken to John, not to the church: “After this I looked, and, behold, a door was opened in heaven: and the first voice which I heard was as it were of a trumpet talking with me; which said, Come up hither, and I will shew thee things which must be hereafter. And immediately I was in the spirit” (Revelation 4:1-2). John was taken up in vision to see what would come, and he came back to write it down for the churches.

2. Sealed before the trumpets

We saw in Part 5 that God seals His servants before the trumpets sound: “Hurt not the earth, neither the sea, nor the trees, till we have sealed the servants of our God in their foreheads” (Revelation 7:3). If God’s people were already gone, there would be no one to seal. The sealing only makes sense if His servants are on the earth when the trumpets begin, and it shows how God protects them: not by removal, but by His mark.

3. The woman in the wilderness, and her remnant

Revelation 12 pictures God’s people as a woman. The dragon, “that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan” (Revelation 12:9), persecutes her, but God carries her to safety: “And to the woman were given two wings of a great eagle, that she might fly into the wilderness, into her place, where she is nourished for a time, and times, and half a time, from the face of the serpent” (Revelation 12:14). Eagle’s wings are God’s own picture of rescue: “I bare you on eagles’ wings, and brought you unto myself” (Exodus 19:4).

As I read it, the woman’s time in the wilderness is the long age of persecution covered in my other papers, the 1,260 years from AD 538 to 1798. Through all of it, God’s people were not taken out of the world. They were “nourished... from the face of the serpent,” kept alive and fed through centuries of trial.

Then comes the last scene in the chapter: “And the dragon was wroth with the woman, and went to make war with the remnant of her seed, which keep the commandments of God, and have the testimony of Jesus Christ” (Revelation 12:17). The remnant of her seed, the last of God’s people, are on the earth, keeping God’s commandments and holding the testimony of Jesus, when the dragon makes his final war.

4. The great multitude out of great tribulation

After the sealing, John saw something wonderful: “After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands” (Revelation 7:9).

One of the elders asked John who they were, and then answered his own question: “These are they which came out of great tribulation, and have washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb” (Revelation 7:14). This is the same “great tribulation” Jesus described: “For then shall be great tribulation, such as was not since the beginning of the world to this time, no, nor ever shall be” (Matthew 24:21).

Notice three things. The multitude is from “all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues,” one company, not two. They “came out of” the great tribulation, which means they were in it. And the promise given to them shows what they endured: “They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more... and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes” (Revelation 7:16-17). They had hungered, and they had wept, and God brought them through.

5. “Behold, I come as a thief,” at the sixth bowl

This may be the most surprising verse for anyone raised on the pre-tribulation view. The idea of Christ coming “as a thief” is the heart of the secret-rapture picture. But look where Jesus says it. The sixth bowl has been poured out, the Euphrates is dried up, and unclean spirits are gathering the kings of the world to Armageddon. Then, in the middle of the bowls, Jesus speaks: “Behold, I come as a thief. Blessed is he that watcheth, and keepeth his garments, lest he walk naked, and they see his shame” (Revelation 16:15).

At the sixth bowl, near the very end, Jesus has still not come as a thief. He is still telling His people to watch and keep their garments. That only makes sense if His watching people are still on the earth during the bowls.

And Paul tells us the thief only surprises those who are not watching: “But ye, brethren, are not in darkness, that that day should overtake you as a thief” (1 Thessalonians 5:4). To the world, Christ comes like a thief. To those who watch, He comes as the One they have been waiting for.

6. “Come out of her, my people”

When Babylon’s judgment is about to fall, a voice from heaven calls: “Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins, and that ye receive not of her plagues” (Revelation 18:4).

God’s people are still on the earth in Babylon’s final hour, and God calls them out so that they will not receive her plagues. This is the Goshen pattern again. God’s people are present when judgment comes, and God separates them from it.

7. One harvest at the last trump

Revelation shows the harvest Jesus spoke of in His parables: “And I looked, and behold a white cloud, and upon the cloud one sat like unto the Son of man, having on his head a golden crown, and in his hand a sharp sickle... Thrust in thy sickle, and reap: for the time is come for thee to reap; for the harvest of the earth is ripe” (Revelation 14:14-15). One harvest of the earth, reaped by the Son of man on the cloud.

When does it come? Paul says the dead are raised and the living changed “In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump” (1 Corinthians 15:52), and “the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16). Revelation numbers the trumpets for us, and the seventh is the last. When it sounds, “The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ,” and “the time of the dead, that they should be judged, and that thou shouldest give reward unto thy servants the prophets, and to the saints” has come (Revelation 11:15, 18). Revelation even uses Paul’s word: “in the days of the voice of the seventh angel, when he shall begin to sound, the mystery of God should be finished” (Revelation 10:7). Paul’s “mystery” of the change at the last trump is finished in the days of the seventh trumpet.

And Revelation names the first resurrection: “and I saw the souls of them that were beheaded for the witness of Jesus, and for the word of God, and which had not worshipped the beast, neither his image, neither had received his mark upon their foreheads, or in their hands; and they lived and reigned with Christ a thousand years... This is the first resurrection” (Revelation 20:4-5). Those who refused the mark of the beast are in the first resurrection. If the church had been raised seven years earlier, that would have been the first. Jesus said it four times in one chapter: “I will raise him up at the last day” (John 6:40; also 6:39, 44, 54).

8. Paul’s own order

Paul wrote to believers who were afraid they had missed the Lord’s coming, and he set the order down plainly: “Now we beseech you, brethren, by the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, and by our gathering together unto him, That ye be not soon shaken in mind, or be troubled... Let no man deceive you by any means: for that day shall not come, except there come a falling away first, and that man of sin be revealed, the son of perdition” (2 Thessalonians 2:1-3).

“Our gathering together unto him” will not come until the man of sin is revealed. And Paul tells us how the man of sin ends: “whom the Lord shall consume with the spirit of his mouth, and shall destroy with the brightness of his coming” (2 Thessalonians 2:8). One coming. One gathering. The man of sin revealed before it, and destroyed by it.

Measured by Scripture

The claim that the church is absent from Revelation’s tribulation chapters conflicts with Scripture:

  • Jesus sent the whole book “unto you... in the churches.”
  • God’s people appear throughout as “the saints” and as those who “keep the commandments of God, and have the testimony of Jesus Christ.”
  • God seals His servants before the trumpets, and nourishes the woman through her long trial.
  • A multitude from every nation “came out of great tribulation.”
  • Jesus says “Behold, I come as a thief” at the sixth bowl, and calls “Come out of her, my people” in Babylon’s last hour.
  • The harvest is reaped at the last trump, those who refused the mark are in the first resurrection, and Paul says our gathering cannot come until the man of sin is revealed.

And through all of it, God keeps His people from His wrath. The bowls fall “upon the men which had the mark of the beast” (Revelation 16:2), and God calls His people out “that ye receive not of her plagues.” The church is on the earth during the tribulation, but it is never the target of God’s wrath. “For God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9).

The great multitude came out of great tribulation. Some of God’s people, in every age, have come out of it only through death. Their story deserves its own part.

Part 8. The Martyrs and the Disciples Who Wait for Us

“They loved not their lives unto the death.” — Revelation 12:11

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view teaches that the church of the last generation will be taken to heaven before the great tribulation, so that it will not have to face the Antichrist’s persecution.

Jesus told His own disciples what was coming

Jesus never promised His followers an escape from suffering. He told them the opposite, plainly and ahead of time: “And ye shall be betrayed both by parents, and brethren, and kinsfolks, and friends; and some of you shall they cause to be put to death. And ye shall be hated of all men for my name’s sake. But there shall not an hair of your head perish. In your patience possess ye your souls” (Luke 21:16-19).

Some of you shall they put to death, and yet not a hair of your head will perish. Both are true at once. Their bodies might die, but nothing of them would be lost to God, who would raise them again. He also warned them, “yea, the time cometh, that whosoever killeth you will think that he doeth God service” (John 16:2). And when James and John asked for the places of honor beside Him, He answered, “Ye shall drink indeed of my cup” (Matthew 20:23).

What Scripture records

Jesus’ words came true for the very men who heard them.

  • Stephen, the first martyr, was stoned outside Jerusalem, praying for the men who killed him: “And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge. And when he had said this, he fell asleep” (Acts 7:60).
  • James, one of the three closest to Jesus, was the first apostle to die: “Now about that time Herod the king stretched forth his hands to vex certain of the church. And he killed James the brother of John with the sword” (Acts 12:1-2).
  • Peter was told by Jesus Himself how he would die: “when thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not. This spake he, signifying by what death he should glorify God” (John 21:18-19).
  • Paul was beaten, stoned, shipwrecked and imprisoned again and again (2 Corinthians 11:24-27), and at the end he wrote: “For I am now ready to be offered, and the time of my departure is at hand” (2 Timothy 4:6).
  • John, the last of them, was exiled for his faith, and he introduced himself to the churches as “your brother, and companion in tribulation, and in the kingdom and patience of Jesus Christ” (Revelation 1:9). The man who wrote Revelation called himself a companion in tribulation.

Notice too that in the same chapter where James was killed, Peter was set free from prison by an angel (Acts 12:7-11). God delivered one and allowed the other to die. Scripture does not promise any believer that he will be spared persecution. It promises that God is with him in it, whatever the outcome.

What church tradition adds

The early church remembered how the rest of the apostles died. What follows comes from early Christian writers and tradition, not from Scripture, and the details vary from one account to another. Tradition holds that Peter was crucified in Rome, and many accounts say he asked to be crucified upside down because he felt unworthy to die as his Lord had. Paul is said to have been beheaded in Rome. Andrew, Thomas, Bartholomew and others are remembered as martyrs in the lands where they carried the gospel. Whatever the exact details, the early church was united in remembering that the apostles laid down their lives for Christ.

The faithful who were not rescued

Hebrews 11 is often called the hall of faith. It begins with great deliverances: Noah, Abraham, Moses at the Red Sea, the walls of Jericho. But then it turns, and names the faithful who were not delivered in this life: “and others were tortured, not accepting deliverance; that they might obtain a better resurrection: And others had trial of cruel mockings and scourgings, yea, moreover of bonds and imprisonment: They were stoned, they were sawn asunder, were tempted, were slain with the sword” (Hebrews 11:35-37).

Then comes one of the most moving sentences in the Bible: “And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect” (Hebrews 11:39-40).

They are waiting for us. Not for a group taken away seven years early, but for all of God’s people to be raised and gathered together.

Revelation shows the same thing. When the fifth seal is opened, John sees the martyrs pictured under the altar, their blood crying out as Abel’s blood cried from the ground (Genesis 4:10): “How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?” They are told to wait “for a little season, until their fellowservants also and their brethren, that should be killed as they were, should be fulfilled” (Revelation 6:10-11).

The martyrs of every age are waiting for their fellow servants, the faithful of the last generation, who will stand where they stood. And of the overcomers at the end, Scripture says what could be said of the apostles: “And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony; and they loved not their lives unto the death” (Revelation 12:11).

Measured by Scripture

The claim that the last generation will be spared the persecution every earlier generation faced conflicts with Scripture. Jesus told His disciples ahead of time that some of them would be killed, and they were: Stephen, James, Peter, Paul, and by the church’s own memory, nearly all the rest. Hebrews 11 honors the faithful who were “not accepting deliverance,” and says they wait so that “they without us should not be made perfect.” The fifth seal shows the martyrs waiting for “their fellowservants... that should be killed as they were.” And Scripture says plainly that God does not play favorites: “For there is no respect of persons with God” (Romans 2:11).

If the apostles who walked with Jesus were not exempt, no generation has been promised an exemption. This is not God’s wrath on His people. It is the world’s hatred, and the dragon’s: “the devil is come down unto you, having great wrath, because he knoweth that he hath but a short time” (Revelation 12:12). God’s wrath falls on the persecutors, never on the persecuted. And Paul, facing his own death, looked forward to a crown he would share with every believer at the same moment: “Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing” (2 Timothy 4:8).

At that day. All together. At His appearing.

Part 9. The Calendar That Never Stopped

“Seventy weeks are determined upon thy people and upon thy holy city.” — Daniel 9:24

This part is brief, because my papers on the seventy weeks and the Continuous Jubilee Calendar cover it in full. But it belongs here, because the seven-year tribulation at the heart of the pre-tribulation view depends on one claim about God’s calendar.

What the view teaches

The pre-tribulation view takes the seven-year tribulation from Daniel 9, the prophecy of seventy “weeks,” or seventy sevens of years. It teaches that sixty-nine of the weeks ran from the decree to rebuild Jerusalem until the Messiah, and then God’s clock stopped. The seventieth week, seven years, was set aside for the future, to begin after the church is removed. In this reading, the “he” who confirms a covenant in Daniel 9:27 is the future Antichrist.

Every count God gave ran straight through

Every time God measured out a span of years for His people, it ran without a break. The time in Egypt: “And it came to pass at the end of the four hundred and thirty years, even the selfsame day it came to pass, that all the hosts of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt” (Exodus 12:41). The years in the wilderness: “each day for a year, shall ye bear your iniquities, even forty years” (Numbers 14:34). The captivity in Babylon: “to fulfil threescore and ten years” (2 Chronicles 36:21). Not one of them stopped partway and picked up again centuries later.

Daniel’s seventy weeks were “determined,” measured out as one span, with nothing in the text to say the clock would stop. And the one who confirms the covenant is the Messiah of the verse before: “And after threescore and two weeks shall Messiah be cut off, but not for himself” (Daniel 9:26). “And he shall confirm the covenant with many for one week: and in the midst of the week he shall cause the sacrifice and the oblation to cease” (Daniel 9:27). Jesus confirmed God’s covenant, and His death in the middle of that last week brought the meaning of every sacrifice to an end.

The Jubilee calendar shows the week fulfilled

God counted Israel’s time in sabbath years and Jubilees, forty-nine-year cycles (Leviticus 25:8-10). Starting from the sign God gave Hezekiah in Part 6, two unsown years in a row, a sabbath year in 703 BC and a Jubilee in 702 BC, the count can be followed with no gaps:

  • 702 BC: a Jubilee, right after the sabbath year of 703 BC (2 Kings 19:29).
  • 457 BC: a Jubilee, five cycles later. Ezra goes up to Jerusalem with the king’s decree “in the seventh year of the king” (Ezra 7:8), and Daniel’s seventy weeks begin.
  • AD 27: Year 43 of its cycle, the first year of the seventieth week, 483 years from the decree. Jesus is anointed: “How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Ghost and with power” (Acts 10:38).
  • AD 30: Year 46, the exact middle of that week. The cross.
  • AD 34: a Jubilee, 490 years from the decree. The seventy weeks are complete.
  • 1798 and 1994: Jubilees, the same forty-nine-year count still running unbroken. 1994 is the seventieth Jubilee on the calendar, the last completed cycle.

Anyone can check it. For a year AD, add 1437; for a year BC, subtract it from 1438. Call that number B. The cycle is (B − 1) divided by 49, dropping the remainder, plus 1. The year within the cycle is B minus (cycle − 1) × 49. Year 1 is a Jubilee. For example, AD 30 gives B = 1467, cycle 30, Year 46, the fourth year of the week of years 43 to 49. The full method is at scripturescjc.com.

The seventieth week does not hang loose in the future. It sits exactly where God’s calendar puts it, fulfilled by Christ, with the same count running unbroken from Hezekiah to our own day. There is no missing seven years left over.

One count, lived through to the end

Daniel’s own end-time numbers point the same way. “And from the time that the daily sacrifice shall be taken away, and the abomination that maketh desolate set up, there shall be a thousand two hundred and ninety days. Blessed is he that waiteth, and cometh to the thousand three hundred and five and thirty days” (Daniel 12:11-12). As I read it, these are literal days at the end, counted from one starting point. And the blessing goes to the one who waits and reaches the end, someone who lives through the whole count.

And God’s own feasts show the order of the harvest. The firstfruits were waved before the LORD first, and the full harvest came after (Leviticus 23:10-11). Paul applies it directly: “But every man in his own order: Christ the firstfruits; afterward they that are Christ’s at his coming” (1 Corinthians 15:23). Christ first. Then everyone who belongs to Him, at His coming. Not in two later stages, seven years apart.

Measured by Scripture

The claim that God stopped the clock after sixty-nine weeks conflicts with Scripture. Every measured count God gave His people ran straight through. Gabriel’s seventy weeks were “determined” as one span. The one who confirmed the covenant was “Messiah... cut off, but not for himself.” And on God’s own Jubilee calendar, the seventieth week lands exactly on Christ’s anointing, His cross in the midst of the week, and the Jubilee of AD 34, with the count running unbroken from 702 BC through 1994.

For the full count, see The Seventy Weeks and CJC Math Lessons at scripturescjc.com.

Part 10. The Hope That Can’t Be Shaken

“And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us.” — Hebrews 11:39-40

We have walked through Scripture’s stories from the flood to the last trump. One question is left, and it may be the most important of all: why does the hope of an early rescue appeal to so many people, and what kind of hope will carry a believer through to the end?

Why the comfortable message is easy to believe

It is natural to want to be rescued before trouble comes. No one wants to suffer, and no one wants to see the people they love suffer. A teaching that promises escape will always be easier to believe than one that promises strength to endure.

Scripture shows that people have always preferred the easier message. Isaiah described a people “Which say to the seers, See not; and to the prophets, Prophesy not unto us right things, speak unto us smooth things, prophesy deceits” (Isaiah 30:10). Jeremiah’s day had prophets “Saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace” (Jeremiah 6:14). Paul warned that the time would come “when they will not endure sound doctrine; but after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears” (2 Timothy 4:3). And he said the end itself would come on people at ease: “For when they shall say, Peace and safety; then sudden destruction cometh upon them” (1 Thessalonians 5:3).

This is not a charge against anyone who holds the pre-tribulation view. Most were simply taught it by people they trusted, and believed it in good faith. It is a reminder that a teaching is not true because it is comforting, any more than it is false because it is hard.

Even Peter wanted the crown without the cross

When Jesus first told His disciples He would suffer and die, Peter could not accept it: “Then Peter took him, and began to rebuke him, saying, Be it far from thee, Lord: this shall not be unto thee.” Jesus’ answer was the sharpest He ever gave a disciple: “Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offence unto me: for thou savourest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men” (Matthew 16:22-23).

Then He told them what following Him would mean: “If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me” (Matthew 16:24). The wish to skip the suffering is a human wish, and Jesus corrected it even in the closest of His disciples.

What faith saves us from, and what it doesn’t

Faith in Christ saves us from everything that truly matters. It saves us from sin’s penalty and from God’s wrath. It saves us from the second death: “He that overcometh shall not be hurt of the second death” (Revelation 2:11).

But Scripture never promises that faith will keep trouble away. “Many are the afflictions of the righteous: but the LORD delivereth him out of them all” (Psalm 34:19). The afflictions come first. Then the deliverance. The apostles taught new believers to expect it: “we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God” (Acts 14:22). And Jesus’ promise to the persecuted church in Smyrna was not a way out, but a crown at the end: “be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life” (Revelation 2:10).

Oil for the long night

Remember the ten virgins. All of them believed the bridegroom was coming. What the foolish lacked was not belief, but oil for a longer wait than they expected: “our lamps are gone out” (Matthew 25:8). And oil could not be borrowed at the last minute.

This is the real danger in expecting an early rescue. A believer who has been promised escape, and then finds the trouble has come anyway, may decide that God has failed him. Jesus warned about exactly this kind of faith: “when tribulation or persecution ariseth because of the word, by and by he is offended” (Matthew 13:21). And He said the end would bring a great falling away: “And then shall many be offended... And because iniquity shall abound, the love of many shall wax cold. But he that shall endure unto the end, the same shall be saved” (Matthew 24:10, 12-13).

A believer who expects to be carried through will not be shaken when the storm comes, because the storm is exactly what he was told to expect. He has oil in his lamp. “Cast not away therefore your confidence, which hath great recompence of reward. For ye have need of patience, that, after ye have done the will of God, ye might receive the promise... But we are not of them who draw back unto perdition; but of them that believe to the saving of the soul” (Hebrews 10:35-36, 39).

Rescue hope and resurrection hope

Here is the difference in a single sentence. A hope built on rescue breaks the moment trouble comes. A hope built on resurrection cannot be broken by anything trouble can do.

The faithful of Hebrews 11 understood this. They were “not accepting deliverance; that they might obtain a better resurrection” (Hebrews 11:35). The three young men in Babylon understood it: “But if not... we will not serve thy gods” (Daniel 3:18). Paul understood it: “For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us” (Romans 8:18).

That is the hope Scripture gives us. Not that we will be spared every trial, but that no trial can take away what God has promised: “For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come... shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38-39).

Faith for the long night

So how do we prepare? Scripture gives us everything we need.

Know the difference between persecution and wrath. Persecution comes from the world and the dragon. Wrath comes from God, and it is never aimed at His people. Jesus prayed “not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil” (John 17:15). God seals His servants before the trumpets (Revelation 7:3), the plagues fall on those with the mark of the beast (Revelation 16:2), and “God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9).

Watch and pray. “Watch ye therefore, and pray always, that ye may be accounted worthy to escape all these things that shall come to pass, and to stand before the Son of man” (Luke 21:36). Jesus had just warned that the day would come “as a snare” on those weighed down with the cares of life (Luke 21:34-35). The escape He promises is from the snare, and the goal is to stand before Him.

Hold on to His promises. “When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee” (Isaiah 43:2). “Bread shall be given him; his waters shall be sure” (Isaiah 33:16). “I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee. So that we may boldly say, The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do unto me” (Hebrews 13:5-6). “And, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world” (Matthew 28:20).

Don’t be surprised, and rejoice. “Beloved, think it not strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you, as though some strange thing happened unto you: But rejoice, inasmuch as ye are partakers of Christ’s sufferings; that, when his glory shall be revealed, ye may be glad also with exceeding joy” (1 Peter 4:12-13).

Run to finish. “Let us run with patience the race that is set before us, Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross” (Hebrews 12:1-2).

Conclusion: Gathered Together in One

We began with one question: when trouble comes at the end, will God take His people out before it, or carry them through it and gather them when Christ appears?

Scripture has answered it from every direction.

  • God has one people, one olive tree, one body, one fold under one Shepherd, and one city with Israel’s tribes on its gates and the apostles on its foundations.
  • Jesus taught one harvest at the end of the world, with the wheat and tares growing together until then, and He told His followers to prepare for a long night.
  • He compared His coming to Noah and Lot, whose deliverance came on the very day of judgment.
  • God kept His people in the fire, in the den, under the decree, in the famine, in Goshen, under the blood, under His mark, inside the walls of Jericho, through the siege and through the storm.
  • Revelation shows His people on the earth, sealed, nourished, watching, called out of Babylon, coming out of great tribulation, and gathered at the last trump.
  • The apostles and martyrs of every age walked this road before us, and they are waiting so that we may all be made perfect together.
  • God’s calendar never stopped. The seventieth week was fulfilled by Christ.

Through all of it, God’s wrath has never once fallen on His people. He has always drawn a line between His people and their enemies, and He always will.

The pre-tribulation view holds on to precious truths: Christ is coming, God’s people are not appointed to wrath, and we must watch and be ready. But measured by Scripture, its central claim, an early removal of the church seven years before Christ’s appearing, does not stand. What stands instead is something better: a God who does not abandon His people to face the storm alone, but walks with them in the fire, feeds them in the famine, shelters them under the blood, and, when the last trumpet sounds, gathers every one of them home.

That is what Caiaphas foretold without knowing it, when he said Jesus would die “not for that nation only, but that also he should gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad” (John 11:52). One people. One harvest. One gathering.

So keep oil in your lamp. Finish the race. And look up, “for your redemption draweth nigh” (Luke 21:28).

“He which testifieth these things saith, Surely I come quickly. Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus” (Revelation 22:20).
